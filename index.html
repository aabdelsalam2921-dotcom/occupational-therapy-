Chapter 1

What is occupational therapy?

Occupational therapy (OT) is the use of exercises, activities, strategies and accommodations to help children develop skills for greater independence.

The word occupation here does not mean only a job. It means every activity that fills a person's day and gives it meaning: getting dressed, eating, learning, playing, caring for others, working and relaxing. When illness, injury, disability or development gets in the way of those activities, an occupational therapist (OT) works out why and helps the person take part again.

The broader international definition, used by the World Federation of Occupational Therapists, describes OT as a client-centred health profession concerned with promoting health and well-being through occupation. Its main goal is to enable people to participate in the activities of everyday life.

Performance versus participation

Performance is how well a child does a task. Participation is whether the child gets to be involved in the life situation at all. OT interventions are not focused only on the child's performance. They also implement adapted methods or apply assistive technology to increase participation despite performance problems.

Worked example

A seven-year-old with weak hand control cannot yet write neatly (a performance problem). The OT builds hand strength through play, and provides a pencil grip, a slanted board and typing access in class. The child joins every lesson now while the hand skills keep improving.

Core idea

Meaningful activity helps people heal and adapt. It grew into a global profession that promotes independence and quality of life.

Nine areas of occupation

The Occupational Therapy Practice Framework (OTPF-4, AOTA 2020) groups human occupation into nine areas. Select a segment.

Five types of activity OT uses with children

1 · Self-care (ADL)

Brushing teeth, buttoning clothes, using eating utensils. Also toileting, bathing and dressing.

2 · Hand-eye coordination

Writing on a classroom whiteboard, copying into a notebook what the teacher writes on the board. Also catching, threading, cutting along a line.

3 · Fine motor skills

Grasping, controlling a pencil, using scissors. Also tying laces, fastening zips, manipulating small objects.

4 · Gross motor skills

Jumping jacks, building core muscle strength for sitting posture. Also balance, climbing and ball skills.

5 · Sensory responses

Helping children with sensory processing issues respond to sensory input in more comfortable ways: textures, sounds, movement, pressure.

Chapter 2

A short history of doing

The profession began with one simple idea: purposeful activity restores health.

  1. Ancient times

    Work and daily tasks as medicine

    Ancient Egyptians and Greeks used work and daily tasks to improve health and well-being. Greek and Roman physicians also prescribed exercise, music, baths and work. The physician Galen is often quoted: "Employment is nature's best physician."

  2. Middle Ages

    Crafts and routines

    Some physicians noticed that engaging in crafts or daily routines reduced stress and improved mental health.

  3. 18th–19th century

    The Moral Treatment Movement

    In Europe and America, doctors began treating people with mental illness through useful activities (farming, handwork, structured routines) instead of isolation and restraints. Key figures include Philippe Pinel in France and William Tuke at the York Retreat in England.

  4. Late 1800s – 1910s

    Arts and Crafts movement and the first practitioners

    The Arts and Crafts movement valued handwork and purposeful making. Early figures such as nurse Susan Tracy, psychiatrist Adolf Meyer and Eleanor Clarke Slagle developed activity-based programmes and habit training in hospitals.

  5. 1917

    The first professional society

    The National Society for the Promotion of Occupational Therapy was founded in the USA. OT was defined as the use of self-care, work and leisure activities as therapy. The society became the American Occupational Therapy Association in 1921.

  6. World War I and II

    Rehabilitating soldiers and civilians

    OT spread widely to rehabilitate injured soldiers and civilians, both physically and mentally. Early practitioners worked as "reconstruction aides".

  7. 1952

    World Federation of Occupational Therapists

    National associations formed the WFOT, which sets international standards for education and practice.

  8. 1960s – 1970s

    Medical model, then a return to occupation

    Practice leaned toward treating body impairments. In 1961 Mary Reilly argued that OT could be one of the great ideas of twentieth-century medicine, calling for a return to occupation. A. Jean Ayres published her sensory integration work in 1972. School-based OT grew after laws guaranteeing education for children with disabilities (in the USA, 1975).

  9. 1980s – 1990s

    Models and occupational science

    The Model of Human Occupation (Kielhofner, 1980), the Person-Environment-Occupation model (Law and colleagues, 1996) and the Canadian Model of Occupational Performance gave the profession its own theories. Occupational science developed as an academic discipline.

  10. Mid 20th century to present

    A global academic and clinical profession

    OT expanded into pediatrics (autism, learning disabilities, physical disabilities), geriatrics (stroke, dementia), hand therapy, neurological rehabilitation and community health.

In summary

Occupational Therapy started from the simple idea that "meaningful activity helps people heal and adapt" and developed into a global profession that promotes independence and quality of life.

Chapter 3

The therapist and where they work

An occupational therapist is a healthcare professional who empowers individuals to lead more productive, satisfying and independent lives. They are often creative in their approach.

Where OT services are provided

Hospitals

Acute care, neonatal units, burns, orthopedics, neurology.

Schools and special schools

Handwriting, classroom access, sensory supports.

Homes

Routines, home safety, caregiver coaching.

Work places

Ergonomics, return to work, job adaptation.

Private clinics

Individual and group therapy, sensory gyms.

Rehabilitation centers

Intensive rehab after stroke, spinal cord or brain injury.

Community health centers

Prevention, group programmes, outreach.

Insurance companies

Assessment, case review and funding decisions.

Chapter 4

Domains of practice

The OTPF-4 organises what OTs look at into five domains and describes the process they follow.

Occupations

The nine areas from Chapter 1: ADLs, IADLs, health management, rest and sleep, education, work, play, leisure, social participation.

Contexts

Environmental factors (physical, social) and personal factors (age, gender, education, lifestyle, culture, values).

Performance patterns

Habits, routines, roles and rituals that shape daily life.

Performance skills

Observable actions: motor skills, process skills (organising, problem-solving), social interaction skills.

Client factors

Values and beliefs, body functions (sensory, neuromusculoskeletal, cognitive, mental) and body structures.

The process

Evaluation

Build an occupational profile (what matters to the client) and analyse occupational performance (what supports or blocks it).

Intervention

Plan with goals, implement with approaches such as prevention, restoration, maintenance, compensation, and review progress.

Targeting outcomes

Measure whether the client achieved participation, well-being, independence, and decide to continue, change or discharge.

Chapter 5

Benefits and referral

Benefits of OT for children

Better concentration

Kids learn to concentrate better and complete their schoolwork with more success.

Self-confidence

Confidence grows as children learn to do more tasks on their own.

Further benefits

Stronger hand and body skills, easier daily routines, calmer responses to sensory input, better play and friendships, practical support for families.

When to refer a child to OT

A child may be referred in two primary situations.

1 · Specific diagnosis

The child has a diagnosis such as autism or cerebral palsy. Others include developmental coordination disorder, Down syndrome, ADHD, spina bifida, muscular dystrophy, brachial plexus injury and global developmental delay.

2 · Functional problem

The child shows issues such as poor fine motor skills or attention deficits, even without a diagnosis. The problem interferes with school, play or self-care.

Signs that may prompt a referral

Hands and school

  • Awkward or tiring pencil grip, messy or very slow writing
  • Avoids drawing, cutting, construction toys
  • Struggles with buttons, zips, laces

Body and movement

  • Clumsy, trips often, avoids playground equipment
  • Slumps at the desk, tires quickly when sitting
  • Fear of swings, heights or uneven ground

Sensory and behaviour

  • Distress with noise, clothing tags, messy textures
  • Very picky eating based on texture
  • Always seeking crashing, spinning, chewing
  • Difficulty staying focused or managing transitions

These signs are prompts for professional assessment. They do not diagnose anything on their own.

Chapter 6

How an OT works with a child

The work follows four steps: comprehensive evaluation, ecologic assessment, context evaluating and effective intervention.

What the OT is looking at

OT interventions do not always focus solely on the child's performance. Five things are analysed together.

  1. Child's behaviours and performance: analysis of actions and abilities.
  2. Environmental influence: how performance is affected by impairment and how the environment supports or constrains it.
  3. Occupations and context: the child's activities and their settings.
  4. Discrepancy identification: gaps between performance and activity demands.
  5. Interrelationship analysis: examining environments, occupations and persons together for clinical decisions.

Step 1 · Comprehensive evaluation

  • Begin by understanding the child's level of participation in daily occupations and routines with family, other caregiving adults and peers.
  • Form a picture of the child that includes his or her interests and priorities.
  • Assess specific performance areas and analyse performance to find the reasons for the child's limitations.

The best strategy for gathering information is to interview the child about play interests, favourite activities, best friends, concerns and worries, using open-ended questions. Establishing a relationship of mutual respect is essential. Understanding the family context and respecting the family's perspective are critical to setting the priorities for intervention and the outcomes most valued.

Step 2 · Ecologic assessment

  • It is the evaluation in the child's natural environment.
  • It lets the therapist see how the environment influences performance and design interventions that are easy to carry out there.
  • It identifies the discrepancy between the child's performance and the expected performance.
  • Careful observation during daily activities (home chores, food shopping) shows which skills to practise, which tasks to modify and which environmental adaptations to make.

It includes: the School Function Assessment and the Sensory Processing Measure.

Ecologic approaches consider the person and the demands the person faces:

  • Physical
  • Social
  • Cultural
  • Temporal

Step 3 · Context evaluating

The therapist evaluates the contexts where the child learns, plays and interacts, such as home, school, daycare and community settings. Performance in several contexts shows how each one affects performance and participation. Physical, social and cultural factors all matter.

    Moving from assessment of participation to analysis of performance and contexts gives the therapist a solid understanding of the strengths, concerns and problems of everyone involved: child, parent or caregiver, family members, teachers. The OT also knows how to interact with the child to build a trusting, positive relationship.

    Step 4 · Effective intervention

    Occupational therapists improve children's performance and participation by providing interventions to enhance performance, adapting activities and modifying the environment, and consulting, educating and advocating. They implement adapted methods or assistive technology to increase participation despite performance problems, while understanding the family context and respecting the family's perspective.

    A · Enhance performance

    Interventions promote and reinforce participation and lead to active engagement. A child's brain responds differently and learns more effectively when actively involved than when receiving passive stimulation. Engagement funnels energy into the activity and helps the child sustain full attention.

    B · Just-right challenge

    To promote change the activity must be challenging and create a degree of stress. Sessions begin with activities the child can do comfortably and competently, then move toward increasing challenges.

    C · Therapeutic relationship

    A relationship that encourages and motivates lets the child feel safe and willing to take risks. The therapist shows interest, enjoys the child's personality, values preferences and goals, respects emotions, and builds a climate of trust and emotional safety.

    Explore the just-right challenge

    Example: a child with gravitational insecurity (fear of and aversion to unstable surfaces or heights, with immature postural balance). Therapy begins close to the ground with close physical support. Gradually, in later sessions, the child walks on unstable surfaces and swings with the whole body suspended. Move the sliders and watch where the child lands.

    Chapter 7

    Rules for choosing activities and encouraging participation

    Rules for applying OT activities

    1. Children experience a greater degree of sensory integration when actively, rather than passively, participating in an activity.
    2. Based on careful analysis of performance and behaviour, the OT selects an activity that matches the child's strengths and limitations across performance domains.
    3. Analysis lets the therapist individualise the difficulty, the pace and the supports a child needs to accomplish a task.
    4. The therapist selects highly adaptable activities that can be modified to raise or lower the difficulty level according to the child's performance.

    How to encourage a child's participation

    Offer choice

    Select an activity of interest that motivates the child and gives the child choices.

    Follow the child

    Encourage positive affect by attending to and imitating the child's actions and communication attempts, then wait for the child's response.

    Connect

    Establish eye contact, use gentle touch, and make non-evaluative comments.

    Grade the activity

    Choose activities that let the child feel important and match the child's abilities. This gives the chance to achieve mastery and a sense of accomplishment.

    Build self-esteem

    Skill achievement and success in mastering a task shape the child's self-esteem and self-image.

    Reinforce

    Provide adequate and appropriate intensity and reinforcement. Children rewarded for performing well show positive effects, so interventions should include reinforcement of effort.

    Reinforcement and feedback

    Natural reinforcement includes eating cookies and enjoying socialisation with others during the activity. Positive reinforcement includes both kinds of feedback:

    Intrinsic

    Feeling competent or a sense of success.

    Extrinsic

    Adult praise or a treat.

    External feedback is effective when it gives the child specific information about performance and when it immediately follows performance. "You held the pencil with three fingers that time" teaches more than "good job" given later.

    Adapted play and technology

    • Adapted play techniques may include switch toys, battery-powered toys, enlarged handles on puzzle pieces and magnetic pieces that fit together easily.
    • Computer programs used in schools to promote literacy, writing and math skills help all children and are particularly beneficial to children with learning and physical disabilities.
    Chapter 8

    The intervention toolkit

    Alongside the process steps, OTs draw on a common set of professional tools.

    Therapeutic use of self

    Rapport, empathy, humour and presence used on purpose to motivate and build trust.

    Activity analysis

    Breaking a task into steps and listing its demands (body, sensory, thinking, social, space, tools) to judge fit with the client.

    Grading and adapting

    Grading changes difficulty step by step. Adapting changes the method, tool or setting so the task becomes possible.

    Assistive technology

    Low tech: pencil grips, built-up cutlery, non-slip mats. High tech: switches, speech devices, eye-gaze systems, adapted computers.

    Splints and orthoses

    Custom devices that protect, support or position hands and joints, common in hand therapy and neurology.

    Education and advocacy

    Teaching families and teachers, recommending accommodations, and speaking up for access and inclusion.

    Chapter 9

    Environmental modification

    A child with disabilities often benefits from modifications to the environment.

    • Enhance participation
    • Increase safety (e.g. reduce barriers on the playground)
    • Improve comfort (e.g. reduce the incline of a ramp)

    Examples in practice

    Accessible bathroom

    The school bathroom may be accessible to a wheelchair user, and the therapist recommends a handlebar beside the toilet so the child can safely perform a standing pivot transfer.

    Noisy classroom

    High noise levels can be overwhelmingly disorganising for a child hypersensitive to auditory stimulation.

    Deep pressure and quiet time

    Young children who need deep pressure or quiet times may need a beanbag chair placed in a pop-up tent in the corner of the room.

    Movable seating

    Sitting on movable surfaces such as liquid-filled cushions can improve posture and attention to classroom instruction, improving arousal and attention.

    More modifications OTs commonly recommend

    • Visual schedules
    • Noise-reducing headphones
    • Slant boards
    • Pencil grips and raised-line paper
    • Footrests so feet reach the floor
    • Non-slip mats
    • Reduced clutter and glare
    • Labelled storage at child height
    Chapter 10

    Child development at a glance

    Knowing typical development tells an OT where a child is and what comes next. Filter by area.

    Gross motorFine motorSelf-carePlay and social

    Ages are approximate typical ranges. Children vary widely, and one late milestone is not a diagnosis.

    Core strength and sitting posture

    Gross motor work in OT includes building core muscle strength for sitting posture. A weak trunk shows up when a child slumps at a desk: the pelvis tips backward, the spine flexes, the neck extends and the chin pokes forward, so the arms bend to reach the work. Upright sitting needs a stable pelvis and trunk before the arms and hands can work precisely.

    Proximal stability supports distal mobility: shoulder and trunk control come first, then fine finger movement.

    How the pencil grasp matures

    1–2 years

    Palmar supinate: fist around the crayon, whole-arm scribbles.

    2–3 years

    Digital pronate: fingers grip, hand faces down, wrist and arm still move the pencil.

    3½–4 years

    Static tripod: thumb, index and middle finger hold, but the whole hand moves.

    4½–6 years

    Dynamic tripod: mature grasp, fingers make small precise movements.

    Chapter 11

    Sensory processing

    Sensory processing is how the nervous system receives, organises and responds to information from the body and the world. It is one of the five main activity areas OTs work on with children.

    Eight senses

    • Sight
    • Hearing
    • Smell
    • Taste
    • Touch
    • Vestibular (movement and balance)
    • Proprioception (body position, joint and muscle input)
    • Interoception (internal body signals)

    Children with sensory processing difficulties may react too strongly, too weakly or unpredictably. Common examples include a child hypersensitive to noise, a child who needs deep pressure, and a child with gravitational insecurity.

    Therapy typically combines rich sensory play (playdough, textured pots, balance discs, tactile mats) with active, goal-driven tasks, because children organise sensation best when they participate actively.

    The evidence for different sensory-based interventions varies. Good practice ties every sensory activity to a functional goal and measures whether it helps.

    Dunn's four sensory patterns

    Passive response
    Active response
    Low threshold (notices easily)

    Sensitivity

    Notices a lot, becomes distressed or distracted.

    Avoiding

    Notices a lot, acts to escape it: covers ears, refuses textures.

    High threshold (needs more)

    Low registration

    Misses input; seems unaware or slow to respond.

    Seeking

    Needs more, acts to get it: spins, crashes, chews.

    Chapter 12

    Frameworks and models

    Models explain what OTs look at and why. This is the overview.

    OTPF-4: Occupational Therapy Practice Framework

    AOTA's framework describing the profession's domain (occupations, contexts, performance patterns, performance skills, client factors) and process (evaluation, intervention, outcomes). See Chapter 4.

    MOHO: Model of Human Occupation

    Developed by Gary Kielhofner. Explains why people do what they do through volition (motivation, interests, values), habituation (habits and roles), performance capacity (body and mind abilities) and the environment.

    PEO: Person-Environment-Occupation

    Law and colleagues, 1996. Occupational performance results from the fit between the person, the environment and the occupation. Improving the fit improves performance. See the diagram in Chapter 6.

    CMOP-E: Canadian Model of Occupational Performance and Engagement

    Shows the person (with spirituality at the centre), the environment and occupations (self-care, productivity, leisure) interacting. Underlies the COPM assessment and client-centred practice.

    ICF: International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health

    WHO framework describing health through body functions and structures, activities, participation, and environmental and personal factors. It underlines the emphasis on participation over performance alone.

    Ayres Sensory Integration

    A. Jean Ayres proposed that learning and behaviour depend on the brain's ability to organise sensation, especially touch, vestibular and proprioceptive input, into adaptive responses. Intervention gives active, playful, just-right sensory-motor challenges.

    Frames of reference
    • Developmental: follows the typical sequence of skills.
    • Neurodevelopmental and motor control: improves movement quality and postural control.
    • Biomechanical: restores range of motion, strength and endurance.
    • Cognitive-behavioural: changes thoughts and behaviours that limit participation.
    • Compensatory and adaptive: changes the task or environment when ability cannot change.
    Client-centred and family-centred care

    The client, and for children the family, sets priorities. This is why understanding the family context and respecting the family's perspective appears in both evaluation and intervention.

    Chapter 14

    Conditions and the whole lifespan

    OT started in mental health and spread to every age group. Today it covers pediatrics, geriatrics, hand therapy, neurological rehabilitation and community health.

    Children and teens

    Cerebral palsy, autism, ADHD, Down syndrome, developmental coordination disorder, learning disabilities, sensory processing difficulties, spina bifida.

    Focus: play, school, self-care, feeding, handwriting, participation.

    Adults: neurological

    Stroke, spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease.

    Focus: retraining daily tasks, upper-limb function, cognition, equipment and home changes.

    Hand and orthopedic

    Fractures, tendon injuries, nerve injuries, burns, amputations, arthritis.

    Focus: splinting, scar management, graded hand use, return to work.

    Mental health

    Depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, trauma, substance use.

    Focus: routines, coping skills, social participation, vocational skills.

    Older adults

    Dementia, falls, frailty, joint disease, stroke recovery.

    Focus: safe independence, home modification, caregiver support.

    Community and work

    Workplace injury, chronic pain, homelessness, refugees, prisons.

    Focus: ergonomics, return to work, access to meaningful roles, occupational justice.

    Chapter 15

    Ethics, evidence and career

    Ethical principles

    • Beneficence: act for the client's benefit
    • Nonmaleficence: avoid harm
    • Autonomy: respect choice and consent
    • Justice: fairness and access
    • Veracity: honesty
    • Fidelity: loyalty and confidentiality

    Evidence-based practice

    Combine the best research, the therapist's clinical reasoning and the client's or family's values. Measure outcomes with standardised tools and goal-based scales.

    Documentation

    Notes record evaluation findings, goals, interventions and progress. Many services use the SOAP format: Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan.

    Career paths

    Clinical practice (pediatrics, hand therapy, neurology, mental health, geriatrics), school systems, community programmes, ergonomics, assistive technology, research, education and management. Entry-level qualifications vary by country.
